Reigning Well

The Reforms of Judah's Righteous Kings — Applied to Inner World and Outer World

By Emi Lokan

This study maps the reforming actions of Judah's faithful kings to both the inner life of the believer and their outward kingdom responsibilities. Each king is read against the charter Moses gave for Israel's kings in Deuteronomy 17, and against the plumb line the biblical historians used to measure them. Kings who later erred are included in full — their failures form part of the teaching.

Every external reform has a corresponding internal confrontation. Every internal transformation should produce visible external fruit. None of this is a programme of self-improvement: the reforms are the fruit of a reign received, not the means of establishing it.

I

Two Jurisdictions

Inner World and Outer World

Throughout this study, inner world and outer world are not loose metaphors for “private” and “public”. They follow the distinction drawn in Scofield's dispensational reading of the Gospels between the kingdom of God and the kingdom of heaven — the same interior jurisdiction that the Bleach ontological mapping locates in Ichigo's Inner World.

Inner World

The kingdom of God — ἐντὸς ὑμῶν, entos hymōn
  • “The kingdom of God cometh not with observation … behold, the kingdom of God is within you” (Luke 17:20–21).
  • In Scofield's terms, “inward and spiritual” (Rom 14:17), entered only by the new birth (John 3:3–5).
  • The hidden throne: who actually reigns over the heart, the will, the appetites and the imagination.

Outer World

The kingdom of heaven — its tangible manifestation
  • In Scofield's terms, “Messianic, mediatorial, and Davidic”, with the object of establishing the kingdom of God in the earth (note on Matt 6:33).
  • The visible sphere: households, congregations, institutions, cultures and nations.
  • Judah's throne was itself “the kingdom of the LORD in the hand of the sons of David” (2 Chron 13:8) — the outer kingdom in its Old Testament form.

The kings of Judah sat on the outer throne. But Scripture never assesses them by the outer throne alone. Every verdict — “his heart was perfect”, “his heart was lifted up” — is a verdict on the inner one. That is why each reform below is read twice: once for what it confronts within, and once for what it makes visible without.

The Bleach mapping names the danger plainly: the inner Hollow does not need to destroy Ichigo, only to take the throne of his inner world. The kings below show the same war fought on Judah's ground.

II

The Measure

“As David his father”

David is not studied as one of the kings here. He appears because the historians of Kings and Chronicles used him as the plumb line against which every son of his line was measured. They were not holding up his household, his wars or his achievements for imitation. They meant one thing by the comparison: a heart wholly true to the LORD (1 Kgs 15:3).

The record refuses to idealise even the measure. It qualifies David's uprightness in the same breath — “save only in the matter of Uriah the Hittite” (1 Kgs 15:5). The plumb line is the covenant loyalty, not the man. And the measure itself points past David to the Son of David, to whom “the Lord God shall give … the throne of his father David” (Luke 1:32–33).

Only three kings are measured against David without qualification: Asa, Hezekiah and Josiah. The wording of Josiah's verdict — turned not aside to the right hand or to the left — is lifted directly from the law of the king. That law is the charter the measure was always pointing back to.

III

The Charter

The Law of the King — Deuteronomy 17:14–20

Before Israel had a king, Moses wrote down what a king must and must not be. It is the only passage in the Torah addressed to the throne directly, and its precepts are the standard behind every verdict in Kings and Chronicles. Each one governs both jurisdictions.

  1. Chosen by the LORD, from among his brethren

    “Thou shalt in any wise set him king over thee, whom the LORD thy God shall choose: one from among thy brethren.” — 17:15

    Inner world
    Authority is received, not seized. The heart's throne belongs to the One who appoints.
    Outer world
    Leaders who stay among the people they serve, not above or apart from them.

  2. Not multiply horses, nor return to Egypt

    “He shall not multiply horses to himself, nor cause the people to return to Egypt.” — 17:16

    Inner world
    Refusing to make strength, strategy or self-sufficiency the true object of trust.
    Outer world
    Not buying security through alliances with the powers God delivered you from.

  3. Not multiply wives, lest his heart turn away

    “Neither shall he multiply wives to himself, that his heart turn not away.” — 17:17

    Inner world
    Guarding covenant faithfulness and an undivided heart; appetite is not allowed to legislate.
    Outer world
    Refusing the political marriages and partnerships that import other gods into the house.

  4. Not greatly multiply silver and gold

    “Neither shall he greatly multiply to himself silver and gold.” — 17:17

    Inner world
    Treasure held loosely; wealth never becomes identity or proof of favour.
    Outer world
    Stewardship over accumulation; resources serve the people, not the image of the ruler.

  5. Write a copy of the law in a book

    “He shall write him a copy of this law in a book out of that which is before the priests the Levites.” — 17:18

    Inner world
    The Word written on the heart by personal labour, not inherited second-hand.
    Outer world
    Governance that is accountable to a standard outside the ruler's will.

  6. Read it all the days of his life

    “It shall be with him, and he shall read therein all the days of his life: that he may learn to fear the LORD his God.” — 17:19

    Inner world
    Lifelong, not seasonal, formation. Faithfulness that outlives mentors and crises.
    Outer world
    Leadership whose consistency over decades becomes its authority.

  7. Heart not lifted up; turn not aside

    “That his heart be not lifted up above his brethren, and that he turn not aside from the commandment, to the right hand, or to the left.” — 17:20

    Inner world
    Humility, especially in success. Pride is the precept most often broken below.
    Outer world
    Staying within one's mandate; neither syncretism to the left nor presumption to the right.

The Deuterocanonical Witness

The deuterocanonical wisdom books take up the same charter and press it on every ruler, reading kingship as stewardship of a kingdom that belongs to God.

“Hear therefore, O ye kings, and understand … For power is given you of the Lord, and sovereignty from the Highest, who shall try your works, and search out your counsels. Because, being ministers of his kingdom, ye have not judged aright, nor kept the law.”Wisdom of Solomon 6:1, 3–4
“The power of the earth is in the hand of the Lord, and in due time he will set over it one that is profitable.”Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) 10:4
“The beginning of pride is when one departeth from God, and his heart is turned away from his Maker.”Sirach 10:12
“All, except David and Ezekias and Josias, were defective: for they forsook the law of the most High, even the kings of Juda failed.”Sirach 49:4

Wisdom calls kings “ministers of his kingdom”: the outer throne is held in trust for the inner reign. Ben Sira's verdict is sterner than that of Kings and Chronicles. Of all Judah's kings, only Hezekiah and Josiah stand beside the measure. The other six kings in this study are counted among the “defective”, and their stories show why.

IV

Asa

The Reforming King — Tearing Down What Was Tolerated

1 Kings 15:11–15 / 2 Chronicles 14–16

Asa was the first king of Judah to pursue aggressive reform. He expelled idols, dismantled high places, and deposed his own grandmother from her position of honour because of her Asherah pole. His early reign is a model of courageous thoroughness. In his latter years, however, he made an alliance with Aram rather than trusting the LORD, and when the seer Hanani rebuked him, he imprisoned him rather than repenting.

VI

Joash (Jehoash)

The Temple Restorer — Faithfulness Under Mentorship

2 Kings 11–12 / 2 Chronicles 23–24

Joash was hidden in the Temple as an infant to save him from Athaliah's massacre. Raised by the priest Jehoiada, he became king at age seven. As long as Jehoiada lived, Joash sought the LORD and organised the repair of the Temple. After Jehoiada's death, however, he abandoned the house of God and even had Zechariah — Jehoiada's son — stoned for rebuking him. His reign exposes the difference between borrowed faith and rooted faith.

VII

Amaziah

The Partial King — Half-Heartedness and Its Cost

2 Kings 14 / 2 Chronicles 25

Amaziah did what was right in the eyes of the LORD, but not wholeheartedly. He dismissed foreign mercenaries at a prophet's instruction and won a great victory. Then he brought back the gods of the Edomites he had defeated and worshipped them. His story is one of the clearest warnings against syncretism, and against the pride that follows obedience when that obedience is not grounded in love for God.

Uzziah (Azariah)

2 Kings 15 / 2 Chronicles 26

Uzziah reigned fifty-two years in Jerusalem. As long as he sought the LORD, God gave him extraordinary success: military victory, agricultural abundance, engineering achievements, and an army equipped with the best technology of the age. When the priests confronted him, he raged and was struck with leprosy on the spot.

Jotham

2 Kings 15:32–38 / 2 Chronicles 27

Jotham is one of Scripture's quietest righteous kings. He did what was right in the eyes of the LORD. He built and fortified. He fought and won. Hezekiah

The Restorer — Reopening What Had Been Shut

2 Kings 18–20 / 2 Chronicles 29–32

Hezekiah came to power after his father Ahaz had shut the Temple doors and set up altars on every street corner. Hezekiah's first act was to reopen and cleanse the Temple. He restored Passover, destroyed the bronze serpent that had become an idol, and trusted God when Assyria surrounded Jerusalem. He uniquely received fifteen extra years of life through prayer. But he showed the treasuries of Jerusalem to the Babylonian envoys, with a pride that brought a prophetic word of judgment on his descendants.

XI

Josiah

The Covenant Restorer — The Book That Changed Everything

2 Kings 22–23 / 2 Chronicles 34–35

Josiah came to the throne at eight years old and by sixteen was actively seeking God. At twenty-six, during Temple repairs, the Book of the Law was rediscovered. When it was read to him, he tore his robes. He then enacted the most thorough reformation in Israel's history, systematically dismantling every form of false worship. No king before or after turned to the LORD with all his heart, soul, and might as Josiah did.

XII

The Limits of the Reformer

Reform Received, Not Achieved

The warnings above are not only about kings who stopped trying. Several of the worst failures in Scripture come from those who tried harder, on their own terms. Reform pursued in one's own strength is not a safer version of the same road. It is a different road, and its failures tend to be larger. Four patterns recur.

The house swept clean, but empty

“When the unclean spirit is gone out of a man… he saith, I will return into my house… and findeth it empty, swept, and garnished. Then goeth he, and taketh with himself seven other spirits more wicked than himself… and the last state of that man is worse than the first” (Matt 12:43–45). An inner world that has been purged but is not governed is not neutral. Joash is this pattern in miniature: the house was in order while Jehoiada lived, and Asherah poles came back when he died. Amaziah is the same: obedient at great cost, then he carried Edom's gods home.

Zeal without a whole heart

Jehu is not a king of Judah but of Israel, and he is the clearest example outside this study. He destroyed Baal worship and was commended for it (2 Kgs 10:28–30), yet he “took no heed to walk in the law of the LORD God of Israel with all his heart” (10:31). Thorough reform can coexist with a heart that was never yielded. That is why Josiah's turning “with all his heart, and with all his soul, and with all his might” (2 Kgs 23:25) is the standard, and not the scale of the demolition.

Beginning in the Spirit, finishing in the flesh

“Are ye so foolish? having begun in the Spirit, are ye now made perfect by the flesh?” (Gal 3:3). Amaziah, Uzziah and Hezekiah each fell at a point of strength: “thine heart lifteth thee up to boast” (2 Chron 25:19); “when he was strong, his heart was lifted up to his destruction” (26:16); “Hezekiah rendered not again according to the benefit done unto him; for his heart was lifted up” (32:25). Perfection attempted in one's own strength turns each success into the next trap.

Carrying everything

“The thing that thou doest is not good. Thou wilt surely wear away, both thou, and this people that is with thee” (Exod 18:17–18). Jotham's record has no major failure partly because it was bounded: he ordered his ways, built what was his to build, and left the high places he could not remove. Even Josiah, the most devoted reformer, ended at Megiddo acting on his own judgement. A reformer who cannot share the load or rest is on the way to one of these other failures.

The tables in this study should therefore be read as the fruit of a received reign, not as a checklist for self-improvement. The inner world here is the kingdom of God, entered by new birth (John 3:3–5) and governed by the Spirit. The actions listed are what that reign produces when it is yielded to. They are not what establishes it. Christ alone is the King who reigns perfectly. Every king in this study fell short, which is why the measure was always pointing past David to his Son (Luke 1:32–33), and why believers reign only “in life by one, Jesus Christ” (Rom 5:17).

XIII

The Governing Principle

Every reform these kings enacted began with a personal reckoning — a moment where the Word of God exposed the gap between what was and what should be. The inner world always precedes the outer. You cannot tear down a high place you have not first seen as an abomination. You cannot restore Passover to a nation you have not first recovered it for yourself.

The pattern of failure is equally instructive. Joash collapses when his mentor dies. Amaziah imports the gods of those he defeats. Uzziah crosses into a priestly function his anointing did not cover. Jehoshaphat makes peace with Ahab to his own cost. Even Hezekiah's pride in the recovery season sowed the seeds of Babylonian exile. The king who reigns well is not the king who never falls, but the king who receives the Word of correction without imprisoning the prophet.

The charter of Deuteronomy 17 was never a list of outward restrictions alone. Horses, wives, silver and gold were each named because each one turns the heart, and a turned heart cannot hold the outer throne. The kingdom of God within is the seat from which the kingdom of heaven is made tangible.

The kingdom of heaven is manifested wherever Christ's reign is yielded to. The king who reigns well is the king whose inner governance is so thoroughly submitted that his outer governance becomes an extension of heaven's order — not his own ambition, fear, or appetite.

“He who rules his spirit is greater than he who takes a city.”Proverbs 16:32
